\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Let $\FF$ denote $\R$ or $\C$.  A \emph{weighted design} of size $m$ and
rank $k$ consists of vectors $g_1,\dots,g_m\in\FF^k$ and weights
$t_1,\dots,t_m>0$ with $\sum_c t_c=1$ subject to the Parseval condition
\begin{equation}\label{eq:parseval}
  \sum_{c=1}^{m} t_c\, g_c^{\vphantom{\mathsf{H}}} g_c\adj \;=\; I_k .
\end{equation}
The condition says that the vectors resolve the identity on average.  Do
$k$ of them resolve it on their own?  The selection problem asks for a
$C\subseteq\{1,\dots,m\}$ with $\lvert C\rvert=k$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dominate}
  \SC \;:=\; \sum_{c\in C} g_c^{\vphantom{\mathsf{H}}} g_c\adj \;\psd\; I_k .
\end{equation}
Such a $C$ \emph{dominates}.  We write $\Gtz(m,k)$ for the
assertion that every design of size $m$ and rank $k$ over $\R$ admits a
dominating $k$-subset, and $\GtzC(m,k)$ for its complex analogue.

At uniform weights the problem is classical.  Let $U\in\FF^{m\times k}$
satisfy $U\adj U=I_k$, let $u_c\adj$ be its $c$-th row, and put
$g_c:=\sqrt m\,u_c$ and $t_c:=1/m$.  Then \eqref{eq:parseval} holds,
the two factors of $m$ cancelling and leaving
$\sum_cu_c^{\vphantom{\mathsf{H}}}u_c\adj=U\adj U=I_k$.  Writing $U_C$ for the
$k\times k$ submatrix on the rows $C$, the same cancellation gives
$\SC=m\,U_C\adj U_C^{\vphantom{\mathsf{H}}}$, so the requirement
\eqref{eq:dominate} reads
\[
  U_C\adj U_C^{\vphantom{\mathsf{H}}}\;\psd\;\tfrac1m I_k,
  \qquad\text{that is}\qquad
  \sigma_{\min}(U_C)\;\ge\;m^{-1/2},
  \qquad\text{that is}\qquad
  \lVert U_C^{-1}\rVert_2\;\le\;\sqrt m .
\]
Some $k\times k$ submatrix of a matrix with orthonormal columns has
inverse of spectral norm at most $\sqrt m$: this is the conjecture of
Goreinov, Tyrtyshnikov and Zamarashkin \cite{GTZ1997}, who raised it while
bounding the error of pseudoskeleton approximation.  It is the analytic
content of the maximal-volume principle, and it remains open.

The principle does prove a weaker bound \cite{GZT1997,GT2001}.
Among the finitely many $k$-subsets choose one of maximal volume, that is
one maximizing $\lvert\det U_C\rvert$.  It is nonsingular: by the
Cauchy--Binet formula
$\sum_{\lvert C\rvert=k}\lvert\det U_C\rvert^2=\det(U\adj U)=1$, so the
volumes do not all vanish.  Put $B:=UU_C^{-1}$.  Replacing the
$j$-th row of $U_C$ by the $r$-th row of $U$ replaces that row by its
expansion $\sum_i B_{ri}(U_C)_i$ in the selected rows; expanding the
determinant linearly in that row leaves only the term $i=j$, every other
having a repeated row, so the new determinant is $B_{rj}\det U_C$.  For
$r\notin C$ that determinant belongs to a genuine $k$-subset, and an entry
of modulus greater than $1$ would name one of strictly larger volume; so
maximality bounds those entries by $1$.  The rows of $B$ indexed by $C$ are
$U_CU_C^{-1}=I_k$, so every entry of $B$ has modulus at most $1$ and the
$k$ rows on $C$ contribute exactly $k$ to the Frobenius norm, whence
\[
  \tr\bigl((U_C\adj U_C^{\vphantom{\mathsf{H}}})^{-1}\bigr)
  \;=\;\lVert U_C^{-1}\rVert_F^2
  \;=\;\lVert B\rVert_F^2
  \;\le\;k+k(m-k),
\]
using $\lVert UX\rVert_F=\lVert X\rVert_F$ in the middle step.  Moreover
$U_C\adj U_C^{\vphantom{\mathsf{H}}}\preceq U\adj U=I_k$, so
$(U_C\adj U_C^{\vphantom{\mathsf{H}}})^{-1}\psd I_k$; a Hermitian $k\times k$ matrix
dominating the identity has all its eigenvalues at least $1$ and so has
largest eigenvalue at most its trace diminished by $k-1$.  Therefore
$\lVert U_C^{-1}\rVert_2^2\le k(m-k)+1$ and
$\SC\psd\frac{m}{k(m-k)+1}I_k$.  The bound and the conjecture differ by a
single term:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:deficit}
  k(m-k)+1\;=\;m\;+\;(k-1)(m-k-1),
\end{equation}
a ring identity in the substitution $k=a+1$, $m-k=b+1$.  The deficit
$(k-1)(m-k-1)$ vanishes precisely when $k=1$ or $m=k+1$, and is positive
otherwise; at $(m,k)=(7,3)$ it equals $6$, and the principle delivers
$\sigma_{\min}^2\ge1/13$ where $1/7$ is asked.  The classical argument
therefore suffices exactly on the range
\begin{equation}\label{eq:elementary}
  k\le1\quad\text{or}\quad m\le k+1 .
\end{equation}

Beyond \eqref{eq:elementary} the known cases are few.  Nesterenko
\cite{Nesterenko2023} settled $(m,k)=(4,2)$ over $\R$ by an argument in
Pl\"ucker coordinates, and reformulated the rank-two problem as an
isoperimetric question for closed polygons \cite{Nesterenko2024}; Sengupta
and Pautov \cite{SenguptaPautov2026} then settled rank two over $\R$ for
every $m$.  Nesterenko also described the equality locus at rank two over
$\R$ \cite{Nesterenko2026real}, and built configurations attaining the
bound $1$ at every pair $(m,k)$ from the star spaces of series-parallel
graphs \cite{Nesterenko2025}.  Over $\C$ the
conjecture is false at rank two: at uniform weights some pair always
satisfies $\SC\psd\alpha I_2$ with $\alpha=2-2/\sqrt3<1$, and $\alpha$
cannot be raised, being attained when $4\mid m$ and approached otherwise
\cite{Nesterenko2026complex}.  The restricted-invertibility theorems
\cite{BourgainTzafriri1987,SpielmanSrivastava2012,MSS2022} select subsets
of size strictly below the rank, with a least eigenvalue controlled by a
stable-rank ratio; that control degenerates as the subset size approaches
the rank, which is where the present question sits.  Maximizing the
volume is itself NP-hard, and NP-hard to approximate
\cite{CivrilMagdonIsmail2009}, so the selection principle asserts
existence without exhibiting a subset, and the algorithmic literature
settles for the polynomial factors of \cite{GuEisenstat1996}.

\medskip

We study the problem with general weights.  The weights then
become a coordinate of the problem rather than a fixed normalization:
vectors and weights vary independently subject to \eqref{eq:parseval},
and the extremal configurations appear as sections over the weight
simplex instead of isolated matrices.  Under the
substitution $v_c:=\sqrt{t_c}\,g_c$ the condition \eqref{eq:parseval} says
that the matrix $V$ with rows $v_c\adj$ is an isometry, and
\eqref{eq:dominate} compares a principal block of the projection
$P=VV\adj$ with the corresponding block of $\diag(t)$.  These coordinates
put the problem on a compact set and open it to variational methods.  At
a fixed pair $(m,k)$ the weighted statement is formally stronger than the
classical one; over all sizes the two agree, since a design with rational
weights is a uniform design of larger size with repeated vectors, and
arbitrary weights follow by a limit.

\medskip

Over $\C$ the problem is settled.  For $k\ge2$ selection succeeds in every
complex design when $m\le k+1$ and fails whenever $m\ge k+2$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:complex}); for $k\le1$ it always succeeds.  Against
\eqref{eq:elementary} this reads: over $\C$ the conjecture holds just
where the maximal-volume count already proves it, and fails everywhere
else.

Nothing remains open over $\C$, and no field-independent argument can
settle the real conjecture.

Over $\R$, rank two holds at
every size (Theorem~\ref{thm:ranktwo}), corank one and corank two hold at
every rank (Theorems~\ref{thm:corankone} and~\ref{thm:coranktwo}), and at
each fixed rank the problem is finite: if $\Gtz(m,k)$ holds for all
$m\le k(k+1)/2+1$ then it holds for all $m$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:window}).
That bound is one more than the dimension of the space of real symmetric
$k\times k$ matrices, the size at which a support-reduction argument
applied to \eqref{eq:parseval} starts to bite.  Rank three leaves the
cells $(6,3)$ and $(7,3)$, and no symmetry reduces the pair further:
$(6,3)$ is self-dual, and duality carries $(7,3)$ to $(7,4)$, again of
corank three (Corollary~\ref{cor:twocells}).

The threshold in \eqref{eq:dominate} is attained at every size
$m\ge k+1$: there are designs in which some $k$-subset dominates and none
dominates strictly (Theorem~\ref{thm:tieseverywhere}).  At corank one
these designs are classified, and they form a section over the whole open
weight simplex, a set of dimension $m-1$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:corankone}).
The regular tetrahedron of Example~\ref{ex:tetra} is one of them; another,
at $(5,3)$, has pairwise non-parallel vectors
(Example~\ref{ex:diamond}), so splitting a vector of a smaller design
does not produce the whole locus.

The space of designs is not compact: its weights range over an open
simplex, and by \eqref{eq:parseval} a vector diverges as its weight tends
to zero.  In the coordinates $(P,t)$ that space becomes the interior of a
compact one, on which the objective
$\max_{\lvert C\rvert=k}\lmin(W[C])$, $W=P-\diag(t)$, extends
continuously.  The smaller cells discharge the added boundary points.
Grant the same statement at $(m-1,k)$ and at $(m-1,k-1)$: then every point
with a vanishing weight carries a dominating $k$-subset
(Theorem~\ref{thm:boundary}), and if $\Gtz(m,k)$ fails the objective
attains its minimum at an interior point, that is at a genuine weighted
design (Theorem~\ref{thm:variational}).  At $(6,3)$ both hypotheses are theorems
of \S\ref{sec:real}; at $(7,3)$ one of them is the cell $(6,3)$.

Rank three then reduces to excluding critical points below the threshold
at the two surviving cells.  \S\ref{sec:interior} derives the system such
a point satisfies: a multiplier $\Lambda$ obeying a quadric law
$g_c\adj\Lambda g_c=\cv$ at every $c$, a coverage law, and three
exclusions that cut down the possible configurations of active subsets.
A congruence relates the objective minimized in \S\ref{sec:compactness}
to the one differentiated in \S\ref{sec:interior}, and the two need not
share their minimizers.  Definition~\ref{def:extremalcex} therefore makes
the step from one to the other a hypothesis, and
Theorem~\ref{thm:rankthree} is the only statement that carries it.

\medskip

Because the threshold is attained, and attained on a set of full dimension
in the weights, no proof of $\Gtz$ can carry a uniform margin: for every
$\varepsilon>0$ there are designs admitting no $k$-subset with
$\SC\psd(1+\varepsilon)I_k$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:nomargin}).  That costs
several familiar strategies.  An induction on the size or on the rank
cannot transport a quantitative slack across a step, there being none to
transport at the source.  A certificate for \eqref{eq:dominate}, whether a
sum-of-squares identity, a dual multiplier, or a determinantal or
interlacing bound, must be exact on the extremal locus, so any strategy
whose output is a strict inequality fails at the outset.  A minimizing
sequence cannot be handled term by term, since the identity available at a
degenerate limit fails at every positive weight, however small
(Theorem~\ref{thm:sharp}); hence the variational principle runs through a
compactification rather than through a density argument.
Appendix~\ref{app:graveyard} collects these strategies with the exact
rational witnesses that refute them.

The cells $(6,3)$ and $(7,3)$ remain open
(Conjectures~\ref{conj:sixthree} and~\ref{conj:seventhree}), and with them
the real problem at rank three and above; over $\C$ the sharp constant at
rank three is bracketed but not determined (Remark~\ref{rem:sharpc}).
\S\ref{sec:open} lists these questions together with what the constraints
above require of any solution.

\subsection*{Notation}

For $x,y\in\FF^k$ we write $\langle x,y\rangle:=x\adj y$, conjugate-linear
in the first argument and linear in the second, and
$\lVert x\rVert^2=\langle x,x\rangle$.  We write $A\psd0$ for a positive
semidefinite Hermitian $A$ and $A\pd0$ for a positive definite one;
$A[C]$ is the principal submatrix on the index set $C$, and
$\operatorname{spec}A$ the spectrum.  A \emph{$k$-subset} always means a
subset of $\{1,\dots,m\}$ of cardinality $k$.  The \emph{corank} of a cell
$(m,k)$ is $m-k$.  A pair $(m,k)$ with $m<k$ carries no design
(Lemma~\ref{lem:span}), so every statement quantifying over such a pair is
vacuous.

never rebound, not even inside a proof.  Symbols outside the table, such
as $x$, $y$, $u$, $A$, $B$, $Y$ and $Z$, are proof-local bound variables;
they are reused across disjoint proofs, and none of them shadows a symbol
of the table.
